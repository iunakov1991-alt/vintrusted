A title correction in Arizona is the formal process of amending an official vehicle title issued...

by the Arizona Department of Transportation Motor Vehicle Division (ADOT MVD) to fix errors in the owner's name, vehicle identification number (VIN), odometer reading, or lienholder information. This administrative procedure is critical because even minor clerical mistakes can create significant legal and financial obstacles, preventing the lawful sale, registration, or financing of a vehicle. The Arizona MVD has specific documentation requirements and forms, such as the Title and Registration Application (Form 96-0236), to initiate a correction, and the process varies depending on whether the current title is in-hand or held by a lienholder. Failing to correct a title error can lead to rejected transactions, insurance claim denials, and complications during a traffic stop or vehicle inspection. Legal Context

The legal authority for title corrections in Arizona resides with the Arizona Department of Transportation Motor Vehicle Division (ADOT MVD), which administers state statutes under Title 28 of the Arizona Revised Statutes. A "title correction" is distinct from a title replacement or a salvage branding; it is specifically for rectifying verifiable data entry mistakes made by the MVD, a dealership, or a previous owner during the titling process. Common correctable errors include misspelled names, transposed digits in the VIN, incorrect odometer disclosures, or inaccurate lienholder details. It is crucial to understand that the Arizona MVD cannot correct a title based on a change of circumstance, such as a name change after marriage, which requires a separate process with supporting legal documents like a marriage certificate. The VIN is the immutable legal identifier for the vehicle, and any discrepancy between the VIN on the title and the VIN physically stamped on the vehicle's dashboard or door jamb must be resolved before any correction can proceed, as it may indicate fraud or a prior rebuilt status. Adherence to the Arizona MVD's precise guidelines is non-negotiable for a successful application.

Step by Step

  1. The step-by-step process for an Arizona title correction begins with identifying the exact error and gathering the required original documents. First, obtain the original vehicle title, the current vehicle registration, and a government-issued photo ID. If the error is on a title held by a lienholder, you must contact the lienholder to request they initiate the correction using a Lienholder's Application for Duplicate Title (Form 96-0586). For an in-hand title, complete the Title and Registration Application (Form 96-0236), clearly indicating the correction needed in Section 3. You must provide documentary evidence to support the correction, such as a prior title showing the correct information, a vehicle sales contract, or an official letter from the dealership that made the error. For a VIN correction, a law enforcement officer or an authorized Arizona MVD third-party provider must complete a Physical Inspection of Vehicle Identification Number (Form 96-0106) to verify the actual VIN on the vehicle. Submit the completed forms, evidence, and the required fee to an Arizona MVD office or authorized third-party office; fees are typically lower if the error was made by the MVD itself. The Arizona Department of Transportation Motor Vehicle Division will then process the application and issue a corrected title, which can take several weeks.

Checklist

  1. Obtain the original Arizona title and current registration from your records or lienholder.
  2. Identify the precise error (e.g., misspelled name, wrong VIN digit, odometer mistake) on the title document.
  3. Contact the Arizona Department of Transportation Motor Vehicle Division (ADOT MVD) or visit their website to confirm the exact form required for your correction type.
  4. For most corrections, complete the Title and Registration Application (Form 96-0236), marking it clearly as a correction.
  5. For a VIN error, schedule a physical VIN inspection with law enforcement or an authorized Arizona MVD inspection provider to complete Form 96-0106.
  6. Gather supporting evidence, such as a purchase contract, prior title, or notarized affidavit explaining the error and the correct information.
  7. If a lienholder holds the title, contact them immediately to coordinate the correction using their specific lienholder form.
  8. Check for any outstanding liens against the vehicle using a VIN check service, as all liens must be satisfied or accounted for before correction.
  9. Verify the odometer reading on the title matches your records and the vehicle's instrument cluster to avoid odometer fraud allegations.
  10. Calculate the correct Arizona MVD fee, which varies if the error was agency-made versus applicant-made.
  11. Submit the complete packet—forms, evidence, original title, and fee—in person at an Arizona MVD or authorized third-party office.
  12. Retain copies of all submitted documents and obtain a receipt for your application.
  13. Monitor the application status online or via phone, as processing can take several weeks.
  14. Upon receiving the new corrected title, immediately verify all information, especially the VIN and your name, for absolute accuracy.
Common Errors

A common and costly error is attempting to use the title correction process to change ownership or alter the legal owner's name without proper supporting court or marriage documents, which the Arizona MVD will reject. Many applicants underestimate the need for original documentation, submitting photocopies of the title or evidence, which results in an immediate denial and delays the process. Title washing is a severe fraud risk where individuals attempt to "correct" a salvage or rebuilt brand off an Arizona title by presenting forged out-of-state documents; the Arizona Department of Transportation Motor Vehicle Division cross-references national databases to prevent this. Failing to resolve a VIN mismatch before submission is a critical mistake, as a physical inspection will reveal the discrepancy and halt the process, potentially triggering a fraud investigation. Applicants often forget to include a lienholder on the application when a loan is active, creating a cloud on the title and violating the security agreement. Another frequent oversight is not verifying the corrected title upon receipt, allowing a residual error to persist into the next transaction cycle.

Fees and Taxes

The fees for an Arizona title correction are not uniform and depend primarily on who made the error. If the mistake was made by the Arizona Department of Transportation Motor Vehicle Division (e.g., a data entry error), the correction fee is often waived or minimal, typically just a few dollars. However, if the error was made by the applicant, a dealership, or a previous owner, the standard duplicate title fee applies, which is $4 for an electronic title and $9 for a physical, printed title. There are additional potential costs: a VIN inspection by law enforcement may be free, but an authorized third-party inspection provider may charge $25-$50. Using an authorized third-party office (MVD Approved Third Party) for submission will incur their service fee, which can add $10 to $30 to the total cost. For titles held by lienholders, the financial institution may charge its own processing fee, which can vary widely. Applicants should also budget for potential notary fees if an affidavit is required and for certified copies of supporting documents like marriage certificates or court orders for name changes, which are separate from the core MVD fee.

VIN Verification

Before initiating any Arizona title correction, conducting a comprehensive VIN verification is a critical first step. This check can reveal if the VIN discrepancy on your title is a simple clerical error or a sign of a deeper issue, such as a vehicle history of salvage in another state, an active lien from a previous owner, or odometer rollback fraud. The Arizona MVD's physical inspection will confirm the VIN on the metal plate matches your documents, but a full history report provides the context needed for your correction application. For vehicles previously titled in other states, a VIN check is essential to uncover "title washing" attempts where a severe brand was removed by moving the vehicle across state lines, a practice the Arizona Department of Transportation Motor Vehicle Division actively investigates. Always obtain a detailed VIN report before submitting correction paperwork to ensure you are correcting a simple error and not inadvertently processing a fraudulent title.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common reason the Arizona MVD rejects a title correction application?

The most common rejection is due to insufficient evidence. The Arizona Department of Transportation Motor Vehicle Division requires original documents or certified copies that irrefutably prove the correct information, such as a prior title or a notarized affidavit from the involved party who made the error.

Can I correct a VIN on an Arizona title if the vehicle was assembled from parts?

No. A VIN correction is only for verifiable clerical errors. If the VIN on the vehicle is not the original factory VIN due to reconstruction or parts swapping, you cannot "correct" it. This becomes a complex rebuilt or specially constructed vehicle title process, requiring multiple inspections and affidavits.

How long does an Arizona title correction take?

Processing times vary. If submitted in person at an Arizona MVD office with all perfect documentation, it can take 2-3 weeks to receive the new title by mail. Submissions by mail or through a third-party office, or those requiring additional review, can take 4-8 weeks.

What should I do if I find a lien on the vehicle during a VIN check?

You must resolve the lien before the Arizona MVD will process any title action, including a correction. Contact the lienholder to obtain a lien release document (Form 96-0588) and submit it with your correction application. An active lien will cause an immediate denial.
